A bad idea and a breakthrough sound the same the day someone says them out loud. The useful question is how to find out cheaply enough that being wrong doesn't hurt.

Picture a 30-person construction firm where a 24-year-old field tech proposes scrapping the expensive project-management software for a shared spreadsheet and a group text. On paper it's a downgrade. In practice it could save the crew hours of admin a day. Experience, pattern recognition and surveys all push you toward no, because people can't ask for what they can't picture yet.

So test it instead of arguing about it. Four weeks, for less than a software trial:

Week 1: write the core assumption as a sentence you can be wrong about, and cap the test budget at a few hundred dollars.
Week 2: run it with one team, include a skeptic, and track one number with a unit.
Week 3: read what the system recorded, not what people said about it.
Week 4: scale, kill or iterate. "Let's keep watching" is not an option.

The takeaway for this week: before your next "no," write down the one number that would change your mind, and give the idea a month to move it. Most ideas die in week 2, cheaply. The few that survive have earned the full investment.
