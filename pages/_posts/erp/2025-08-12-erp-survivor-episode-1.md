**Sarah** *(typing fast)*: "I'm listing all 247 of our templates. Should I add the backup copies of the backup copies?"

**Linda**: "Of course. And it must keep our color-coding. Red is rush, yellow means I need to review it, and green means... we are not sure, but we have used it for twelve years."

> **Commercial break — the lesson:** Spreadsheet sprawl is not a quirk. It is a broken control. Two-hundred-plus templates with no version control means nobody can prove which number is right at close. The goal of ERP is not to rebuild every spreadsheet inside the software. It is to decide which numbers are the real ones, who owns them, and where they live. "Make it work like Excel" is the ask that sinks the project.

### Operations tribe panic mode

**Bob**: "The ERP people say we cannot change the inventory module to match our system just as it is."

**Carol**: "But what about our process for back-ordered items that were pre-ordered, then re-ordered during the busy-season tweak?"

**Mike**: "The data move will handle it. How hard can moving 30 years of data be, from a system changed by seventeen coders who all left without notes?"

**Bob** *(staring into the distance)*: "We had a good system. A simple system. You could fix it with a screwdriver and a can of compressed air."

> **Commercial break — the lesson:** Every unwritten workaround is a trap for data moves. Before you move a single record, list the custom changes and ask which ones match a real business rule versus a habit that grew around a system limit. Most of Carol's 47 workarounds go away when the old limit does. The ones that last become setup, not custom code.

### IT tribe's too-sure hour

**Jennifer**: "Once we go live, all our data will flow between teams and create one source of truth."

**Tom** *(consulting his binder)*: "Per the playbook, Phase 1.3.2.B states 47% speed gains in the first quarter."

**Alex**: "If anything breaks, I'll write a custom connector. A weekend, two tops."

> **Commercial break — the lesson:** Vendor playbooks are useful, but the published numbers are sales talk, not a forecast for your shop. Treat every "tops, two weekends" connector estimate as the best-case end of a range and double it. The teams that win plan for the connectors that will not work on the first try.

### The plot twist: reality strikes

**Host**: "Today's guest judge is a real ERP consultant who has seen this before."

**Guest Judge** *(chuckling)*: "Let me guess. Finance wants the system to be Excel. Operations wants nothing to change. IT thinks this wraps in six months with no issues."

*Awkward silence.*

**Guest Judge**: "First reality check. Every one of you will change how you work. Your templates will go stale. Your workarounds will become standard features. And it will take longer and cost more than you planned."

**Linda** *(gasping)*: "But... my macros!"

**Bob** *(muttering)*: "I knew this was a bad idea."

**Jennifer**: "But the speed gains!"

### The alliance forms

**Narrator**: "Faced with the facts, odd teams form..."

*Linda and Bob, huddled in the break room.*

**Linda**: "Bob, I know we have fought about month-end matching, but we need to stick together."

**Bob**: "They want to wipe out everything we built. My inventory system is old, but it works."

**Linda**: "And my reports are dense, but bosses see just what they need."

**Bob**: "So we push for few changes?"

**Linda**: "Deal. The Alliance of the Spreadsheet People is born."

> **Commercial break — the lesson:** This pact is the moment most rollouts die. When your most skilled people quietly agree to push back, they keep the old systems alive in the shadows, and the new ERP never becomes the system of record. The fix is not to overrule Linda and Bob. It is to bring them in. Make them the ones who design the new process, and the pact flips from blocker to backbone.

## How a real rollout plays out

You do not win this game with software. You win it with a phased plan that gives doubters a job.

1. **Look and map the work (2-4 weeks).** Write down how work really happens, not how the org chart says it should. This is where Linda's 247 templates get sorted into "keep as a report," "becomes a system function," and "should never have existed."
2. **Design and setup (4-8 weeks).** Use the vendor's standard chart of accounts and workflows where they fit; set up, do not rewrite, unless a real business rule needs it. Microsoft's own [Dynamics 365 implementation guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/guidance/implementation-guide/overview) is a solid primary source for what a tight method looks like.
3. **Data move and checks (3-6 weeks, at the same time).** Move in waves, match each wave against the old system, and have Bob sign off on inventory counts. The data move is the step that always runs long.
4. **User tests and training (2-4 weeks).** Real users run real entries before go-live. Make the spreadsheet holdouts your testers; if it gets past Linda, it will get past anyone.
5. **Go-live and settle-in (4-8 weeks).** Plan for a slower close the first cycle, then faster every cycle after. Drop the old spreadsheets on a date, in writing, so nobody runs both side by side.

## Watch-outs that take out players

- **Side-by-side systems.** If the old spreadsheets keep running "just in case," the ERP becomes a second job, not the new home. Set a drop date and stick to it.
- **Changing the system to keep old habits.** Every custom change is a cost you pay again at the next upgrade. Default to standard setup.
- **Skipping the people work.** The software installs in weeks. Buy-in takes a quarter. Budget time for training, backing from an owner or controller, and a few in-house backers.
